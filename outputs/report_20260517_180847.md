# Final Report: Analysis of Colorado Initiative 85

---

## 1. Bottom Line

Colorado Initiative 85 encompasses two distinct proposals sharing the same numeric label but addressing very different policy domains: (1) a criminal justice initiative focused on increasing felony classifications and modifying sentencing related to fentanyl and synthetic opioids, along with mandating treatment provisions; and (2) an energy consumer choice initiative aimed at preventing government regulations that restrict consumer choice of energy sources for heating, cooling, and related uses.

The fentanyl-focused Initiative 85 proposes tougher felony penalties, reduced sentencing exemptions, and mandated treatment, aiming to strengthen legal responses to the opioid crisis. Official sources project increased state and local government expenditures due to longer prison sentences and expanded judicial caseloads. However, evidence supporting incarceration effectiveness in reducing fentanyl offenses is limited, and expert concerns caution about disproportionate impacts on marginalized communities and the initiative’s predominantly punitive orientation rather than a comprehensive public health approach.

The energy-focused Initiative 85 seeks to protect individual consumer rights and economic flexibility in energy choices by restricting regulatory authority of state and local governments. Supporters emphasize consumer freedom, while opponents warn it could hinder local climate change efforts and renewables adoption. Official fiscal summaries note likely procedural cost increases related to housing and building code revisions.

Given differences in policy domain, evidence bases, and uncertainty levels, this report treats these two initiatives separately, summarizes evidence-based findings, highlights tentative insights, and outlines key gaps and trade-offs for policymakers’ consideration.

---

## 2. Evidence-Supported Findings

### Fentanyl-Related Initiative 85 (Criminal Justice)

- The initiative **increases felony classifications** for distribution, manufacturing, dispensing, sale, or possession of fentanyl and certain synthetic opioids, as stated in official Colorado Secretary of State documents (Evidence Status: Strong, Confidence: High).

- It **mandates treatment** for certain drug felony violations based on possession amounts, as explicitly described in official initiative language (Strong, High).

- The initiative **modifies sentencing provisions** by narrowing or eliminating exemptions for fentanyl-related crimes and drug-related deaths (Strong, High).

- Projected increases in **state expenditures** are approximately $856,770 in FY 2026-27 and nearly $5 million in FY 2027-28, attributed to longer prison sentences (Limited Evidence, High Confidence).

- The initiative could lead to **increased local government costs and caseloads** for prosecution, law enforcement, and judicial systems; however, these costs are not precisely quantified and vary by jurisdiction (Limited Evidence, Medium Confidence).

- Official critiques and analyses find **the initiative disproportionately emphasizes punitive measures** over public health strategies such as prevention, education, and rehabilitation (Theory-Informed, Rigorous, High Confidence).

### Energy Choice Initiative 85

- The initiative **aims to prevent state and local government regulations** that restrict consumer choice of energy sources for cooking, heating, cooling, and hot water systems (Strong, High).

- Supporters argue that it **protects individual rights and economic flexibility**, ensuring consumer freedom to select energy sources without government interference (Weak, Speculative, High Confidence for claim existence).

- Opponents fear it could **impede climate change efforts and renewable energy adoption** by restricting local regulatory authority (Weak, Speculative, High Confidence for claim existence).

- The initiative may result in **increased costs for state and local governments related to housing and building code revisions**, as indicated by legislative fiscal summaries (Limited Evidence, High Confidence).

---

## 3. Evidence Quality and Source Support

- Most core claims about the legal reclassification, sentencing changes, and mandated treatment provisions of the fentanyl initiative are **strongly supported by official state documents** with high confidence, but lack outcome or implementation data.

- Fiscal cost projections derive from **official legislative fiscal impact reviews**, considered limited evidence due to dependence on assumptions and modeling without observed data.

- Critiques of effectiveness, equity impacts, and public health balance are **theory-informed and lack direct empirical validation**, thus presented as limited or weak evidence with medium confidence.

- Claims about the energy initiative’s goals and stakeholder arguments are based on **official summaries and advocacy statements**, treated as speculative evidence with high certainty that these views exist, but no confirmed empirical outcomes.

---

## 4. Exploratory or Tentative Insights

- Increased felony penalties and reduced sentencing exemptions may push fentanyl-related activities into more hidden or alternative markets, complicating enforcement efforts.

- Mandated treatment holds potential to reduce recidivism and opioid dependency if adequately resourced and implemented in an accessible, non-punitive manner; without these conditions, it risks being ineffective.

- Rising felony classifications and longer sentences might exacerbate strain on prison and judicial systems, potentially lowering enforcement quality or accelerating plea bargaining, undermining deterrence.

- The punitive emphasis may neglect broader public health strategies essential to addressing root causes of opioid addiction, limiting long-term effectiveness.

- For the energy initiative, restricting local regulatory capacity may reduce innovation tailored to local conditions and slow renewables adoption, potentially leading to uneven environmental outcomes.

- Both initiatives risk disproportionately affecting marginalized communities, risking exacerbation of existing inequalities without targeted mitigations.

---

## 5. Key Evidence Gaps

- Lack of high-quality empirical studies assessing causal impacts of felony reclassification and mandatory treatment on fentanyl overdoses and trafficking rates in Colorado or comparable jurisdictions.

- Data on actual implementation quality, capacity, and outcomes (e.g., treatment retention and relapse rates) following mandated treatment provisions.

- Quantitative analyses of racial and socioeconomic disparities in sentencing and treatment outcomes under new felony classifications.

- Empirical measurements of increased local government law enforcement and judicial caseloads and associated budget impacts.

- Long-term environmental and economic impacts of the energy consumer choice initiative, including effects on renewable energy adoption.

- Comparative evaluations of similar legal and energy policies in other states to inform expected outcomes.

---

## 6. Relevant Systems Effects and Unintended Consequences

- **Correctional and Judicial System Feedback Loop:** Higher felony penalties and longer sentences increase prison populations and judicial caseloads, driving up costs and possibly diverting public funds from health services that could address addiction.

- **Marginalized Communities:** Increased penalization and mandated treatment may disproportionately impact lower-income and minority populations, exacerbating systemic disparities.

- **Local Government Capacity:** Smaller jurisdictions may face disproportionate burdens managing increased caseloads, risking enforcement gaps or degraded service quality unless supported.

- **Energy Regulatory Trade-offs:** The energy initiative’s restrictions on government regulation could lead to fragmented energy markets, consumer confusion, and possibly slower progress on climate goals due to lack of local adaptation.

---

## 7. Tradeoffs

- **Public Safety vs. Public Health:** Tougher penalties may increase incarceration costs and social harms without clear evidence of reducing fentanyl use or deaths. Balancing criminal justice actions with robust health and social interventions is crucial.

- **State vs. Local Control:** Energy initiative supporters prioritize individual consumer choice and economic flexibility; opponents emphasize local authority’s role in environmental stewardship and climate responsiveness.

- **Fiscal Costs vs. Benefits:** Increased state and local expenditures on prisons, courts, and housing/building code adjustments must be weighed against uncertain benefits in crime reduction or energy market outcomes.

- **Equity vs. Universality:** Mandated treatments and penalties could raise equity concerns; policies need tailored measures to prevent disproportionate burdens on vulnerable groups.

---

## 8. Limitations

- The report relies on Colorado state official documents, critiques, and fiscal projections which inherently contain assumptions and lack empirical outcome data.

- Evidence on treatment effectiveness, incarceration impacts, and equity considerations is largely indirect or theory-informed rather than based on direct empirical studies.

- Environmental impact claims related to the energy initiative are based primarily on opponents’ projections without direct measurement.

- The coexistence of two unrelated initiatives under the same number (85) poses risks of confusion; care was taken to separate analyses.

# Summary

Colorado Initiative 85 (fentanyl-related) proposes increased felony classifications, mandated treatment, and sentencing reforms expected to raise correctional costs and law enforcement caseloads, but with limited evidence supporting incarceration as an effective deterrent. Critics emphasize the initiative’s punitive focus over public health strategies and caution against disproportionate impacts on marginalized populations.

Separately, the energy version of Initiative 85 aims to preserve consumer choice in energy sources by limiting government regulation, provoking debate over economic freedom versus environmental goals.

Decision-makers should carefully weigh these legal, fiscal, public health, equity, and environmental trade-offs, recognizing evidence strengths and gaps, and consider complementary strategies to enhance treatment, support local governments, and ensure equitable and effective policy outcomes.

### Notes on Consensus Process and Use

- **Conservatism**: Where reviewers disagreed, the lower evidence level or certainty was adopted (except where more rigorous rating clearly justified).
- **Descriptive claims** strictly cover official document content or projections without causal or outcome evidence.
- **Level 1 ratings** reserved to weak, advocacy-based claims without demonstrated outcomes; not to be presented as strong evidence.
- **Level 2 ratings** encompass theory-informed insights and official projections with moderate confidence but acknowledge data limitations.
- **Level 3 reserved** for well-supported descriptive document analysis characterizing policy focus, without causal impact data.
- **Human review flagged** for fiscal projections affecting state expenditures and energy initiative fiscal impacts due to modeling reliance and uncertainty.
- **Claims on incarceration effectiveness and equity concerns** assigned Level 2 limited evidence given plausible theory but insufficient empirical data.
- **Recommendations and report language** should reflect these evidence levels conservatively, emphasizing uncertainties and evidence gaps.

This consensus framework ensures balanced, transparent communication of evidence quality for informed policymaker decisions.

### 3. Which Disagreements Matter Most

- **Fiscal Impact Evidence Levels:**  
  The most influential disagreement concerns whether *projected fiscal impacts* (especially state expenditures and energy initiative costs) qualify as evidence (Level 2) or remain descriptive only. These influence how strongly the report signals budget impact confidence. Given that projections rely on modeling rather than observed data, this judgment affects report tone on financial certainty.

- **Effectiveness and Equity Claims (Level 1 vs. Level 2):**  
  The classification of critiques and equity risks as either weak/speculative or theory-informed limited evidence determines whether these are framed mainly as concerns or as tentative findings deserving more weight. This impacts policy recommendations and calls for evaluation.

- **Policy Focus Critique (Level 2 vs. 3):**  
  Whether the punitive vs public health focus critique is described as moderately supported or rigorously supported affects how decisively the report characterizes the initiative’s orientation.

### 4. Evidence Ratings Requiring Human Review

- **Fiscal projections:**  
  Reviewers vary between Level 2 and No Level/Descriptive with high certainty. Because fiscal impacts are central to decision-making and modeling assumptions are critical, a human expert should review whether these claims meet minimal evidence standards or are purely descriptive projections.

- **Effectiveness of incarceration and equity concerns:**  
  Human review needed to judge whether theory-informed, limited evidence (Level 2) is justified or if these should remain weak/speculative (Level 1). The distinctions influence emphasis on uncertainty and research needs.

  Since Reviewer C rated as descriptive only, while others rated Level 2, a human should clarify how to treat official fiscal summaries that project procedural costs without observed data.

- **Punitive vs. public health focus rating:**  
  Human evaluation could assess if the critique basis truly warrants Level 3 (rigorous) designation or if Level 2 better reflects available evidence.

### Summary Notes:

- **No Level - Descriptive only:** Applied strictly to factual statements derived directly from official initiative language, fiscal reports, or straightforward policy descriptions with no outcome or causal evaluations.

- **Level 1 (Weak/speculative):** Used for stakeholder arguments and advocacy claims lacking independent empirical evidence for impact; these claims reflect views but not tested effectiveness.

- **Level 2 (Theory-informed, limited):** Applied where plausible insights or concerns arise based on theory, expert opinion, or basic descriptive analysis, with limited or no empirical quantification—appropriate for highlighting uncertainties or potential equity issues.

- **Level 3 (Theory-informed, rigorous):** Used to indicate well-supported descriptive or document analysis findings where evidence supports a characterization of policy emphasis (e.g., punitive vs public health focus) but without causal outcome data.

- **No claims met Level 4 or 5:** No causal evidence from controlled studies or strong quasi-experimental analyses found for effectiveness or impact of the initiatives.

### Recommendations for Report Use:

- Use **descriptive-only claims** as trusted factual background on what the initiatives propose or estimate in costs.

- Use **level 1 claims** only to reflect viewpoints and arguments from supporters or opponents, clearly noting the lack of empirical basis.

- Use **level 2 claims** as important exploratory insights to inform caution, identify gaps, and guide future evaluation priorities.

- Use **level 3 claims** to provide evidence-supported findings where clear descriptive patterns or policy contrasts emerge from analysis.

- Avoid causal language except where sturdy RCT or quasi-experimental evidence supports it; none found here.

This approach ensures policymakers receive clear, balanced information calibrated to evidence quality and confidence.
