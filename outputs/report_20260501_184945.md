
# Final Report

**Final Report: Colorado 2026 Fentanyl Penalty Ballot Measure**

---

### 1. Bottom Line

A November 2025 ballot measure proposes significantly increased felony penalties for fentanyl possession, manufacturing, and distribution in Colorado’s 2026 general election. While designed to address a serious fentanyl overdose crisis documented by nearly 1,100 deaths in 2023, the measure carries substantial projected increases in prison population and state costs without corresponding new funding for substance use treatment. Existing research and stakeholder perspectives raise concerns about the potential efficacy and equity of punitive approaches and highlight risks related to treatment access and public health outcomes. Policymakers should weigh these findings cautiously, acknowledging that the measure’s impact remains unproven and contingent on implementation details and additional system support.

---

### 2. Evidence-Supported Findings

- **Ballot Measure Submission and Provisions:**  
  - A coalition submitted over 200,000 signatures in November 2025 to place the measure on Colorado’s 2026 general election ballot (CPR, 2025; Colorado Politics, 2025).  
  - The measure would criminalize possession of any amount of fentanyl as a felony, while manufacturing or distributing fentanyl would become a Class 1 felony carrying up to 32 years imprisonment (Colorado Politics, 2025).  
  - Individuals caught with up to 1 gram of fentanyl may be eligible for court-ordered substance use treatment instead of incarceration; however, eligibility criteria and program capacity are unspecified (Colorado Politics, 2025).

- **Context of Overdose Crisis:**  
  - The state experienced nearly 1,100 fentanyl-related overdose deaths in 2023, indicating a worsening public health emergency (Colorado Politics, 2025).

- **Fiscal and Incarceration Projections:**  
  - Official legislative estimates forecast an increase in the prison population by approximately 900 individuals annually due to the measure, with an estimated $856,770 cost to the state in the first year (Colorado Legislative Fiscal Impact Statement, 2026).

- **Treatment Funding and Access:**  
  - The measure does not allocate new resources to expand substance use treatment. Given Colorado’s documented treatment access shortages, this risks exacerbating waitlists and limiting meaningful diversion from incarceration (CCJRC, 2025).

- **Positions of Stakeholders:**  
  - Supporters argue that harsher penalties are necessary to hold drug dealers accountable and address fentanyl’s deadly proliferation (Colorado Politics, 2025).  
  - Opponents, including some Democrats and advocacy groups, express concern that punitive measures could disproportionately harm individuals with addiction and advocate instead for treatment- and harm-reduction-focused strategies (CPR, 2025).

- **Previous Legislative Attempts:**  
  - A similar 2024 bill (House Bill 24-1306) seeking tougher synthetic opioid penalties failed in the Colorado General Assembly, illustrating political contention around such measures (Colorado General Assembly, 2024).

- **Research on Policy Effectiveness:**  
  - Broader studies indicate that increased incarceration and harsher drug penalties generally do not reduce drug use or overdose deaths and may deter overdose assistance, again raising questions about this measure’s likely impact (CCJRC, 2025; medium confidence).

---

### 3. Evidence Quality and Source Support

- The core factual claims—ballot submission, measure provisions, overdose statistics, and fiscal projections—are supported by multiple credible media and government sources with **high certainty**. These are appropriately reported as descriptive, not evaluative claims.  
- Stakeholder opinions from supporters and opponents are consistent but constitute **Level 1 (weak/speculative) evidence**, reflecting advocacy positions without independent empirical substantiation.  
- The research evidence warning about punitive policies’ limited effectiveness is **Level 3 (theory-informed, rigorous)** but with **medium certainty** given lack of direct causal studies specific to this measure.  
- Fiscal impact projections and treatment access concerns are **Level 2 (theory-informed, limited)** evidence—supported by official data and plausible inference but not confirmed by observed outcomes.  

---

### 4. Exploratory or Tentative Insights

- If implemented, the measure could shift court priorities and system resources towards prosecuting low-level possession cases as felonies, potentially straining judicial and correctional capacity.  
- Eligibility for court-ordered treatment up to 1 gram possession may be limited by current treatment infrastructure, potentially undermining rehabilitative intent and defaulting to incarceration.  
- Increased incarceration costs might reduce available funding for treatment and prevention, creating a counterproductive feedback loop.  
- Harsh penalties might reduce users’ or bystanders’ willingness to call emergency services during overdoses due to fear of felony charges, complicating public health responses.  
- The measure may intensify political polarization around drug policy in Colorado, influencing future legislative and budgetary initiatives.  

---

### 5. Key Evidence Gaps

- Lack of definitive data on how eligibility for court-ordered substance use treatment will be operationalized and resourced.  
- Absence of explicit evidence evaluating the direct causal impact of similar penalty increases on fentanyl use rates, overdose deaths, or treatment engagement in Colorado or comparable jurisdictions.  
- Uncertainty around how the legal system will handle varied prosecutorial discretion and judicial decisions in applying the new felony classification.  
- No planned monitoring or evaluation mechanisms detailed to assess measure effectiveness post-implementation.  

---

### 6. Relevant Systems Effects and Unintended Consequences

- Potential increase in incarcerated population with ramifications on prison capacity, staffing, and budgets.  
- Risk of net-widening, criminalizing low-level users who might otherwise receive diversion or lower-level penalties.  
- Strain on already limited substance use treatment resources, possibly increasing wait times and undermining rehabilitation efforts.  
- Economic tradeoffs with incarceration spending potentially limiting investment in public health and prevention programs.  
- Possible reduction in overdose reversals and harm reduction engagement due to fear of felony charges.  

---

### 7. Tradeoffs

- The measure prioritizes **punitive enforcement and accountability**, supported by advocates responding to a lethal fentanyl crisis. This may increase deterrence in theory but risks exacerbating incarceration and undermining public health.  
- It incorporates a limited **treatment eligibility provision**, yet without funding increases, this may only partially offset incarceration increases or fail in implementation.  
- The measure could strengthen law enforcement tools but at potentially high **financial and social costs**, affecting both individual outcomes and systemic resources.  
- Opponents favor **treatment-focused, harm-reduction policies**, arguing these align better with addiction science and reduce collateral harm but may be viewed as politically less decisive by some voters.  

---

### 8. Limitations

- The measure’s official wording and legal implementation details are not finalized and may alter provisions and effects.  
- Fiscal and prison population estimates are projections relying on assumptions; actual impacts may vary.  
- Research on punitive policy impacts is drawn from broader literature and may not translate directly to Colorado’s specific legal and social context.  
- Stakeholder advocacy positions reflect perspectives rather than independent evidence on outcomes.  
- Data on overdose deaths come from 2023 and do not predict future trends or policy effects.

# Appendix E: Systems + Multidisciplinary Analysis

**1. Exploratory Insights (Tentative / Hypothesis-Generating):**

- **Criminalization as Deterrence vs. Addiction Complexity:** While supporters view harsh penalties as a lever to curb fentanyl supply and use, the addictive nature of opioid use might blunt deterrence effects. The measure’s criminalization could inadvertently push users deeper into underground markets or riskier behaviors, reducing contact with treatment and harm reduction services.

- **Treatment Eligibility as a Gatekeeper:** Eligibility for court-ordered treatment up to 1 gram possession introduces conditional leniency. However, if courts and treatment programs lack capacity or clear guidelines, this provision may function more symbolically than practically, potentially funneling many into incarceration regardless.

- **Political Messaging and Voter Behavior:** The ballot initiative’s framing as a tough-on-drugs approach may galvanize certain voter bases, notably more conservative or moderate groups concerned with public safety. However, it may alienate public health-oriented voters without concurrent investment in treatment, impacting broader drug policy reforms in Colorado.

- **Resource Allocation Feedback Loop:** Increased incarceration costs (~$857K first year) might divert funds from treatment and prevention if the state budget is constrained, potentially exacerbating the fentanyl crisis through under-resourced public health infrastructure.

**2. Evidence-Supported Insights:**

- **Measure Details and Scope:** The Colorado ballot measure proposes felony penalties for any fentanyl possession and severe penalties (up to 32 years) for manufacturing or distribution (evidence #1, #2). This represents one of the strictest stances nationally on fentanyl offenses.

- **Fiscal and Capacity Impact:** The measure is projected to increase the state prison population by about 900 annually, with near $857,770 in estimated initial costs (evidence #6). This signals a substantial immediate system burden.

- **Treatment Option Limited and Underfunded:** Although court-ordered treatment is an option for those possessing up to 1 gram, no new funding is allocated to expand treatment capacity, despite documented shortages in Colorado (evidence #3, #9, #10).

- **Mixed Positions Reflect Policy Divide:** Supporters emphasize accountability and overdose crisis urgency (evidence #11), while opponents warn of disproportionate impacts on addicted individuals and advocate for treatment/harm reduction (evidence #5).

- **Historical Legislative Resistance:** Prior attempts to elevate synthetic opioid penalties failed in the legislature (House Bill 24-1306 in 2024), suggesting political contention or concerns about harsher punitive approaches (evidence #7).

- **Overdose Crisis Severity:** Nearly 1,100 fentanyl-related deaths in Colorado in 2023 highlight the urgency fueling the initiative (evidence #4).

- **Questionable Policy Efficacy:** Broader research cautions that harsher penalties alone often fail to reduce drug use or overdose mortalities and may inadvertently deter life-saving interventions like overdose assistance (evidence #8, medium confidence).

**3. Plausible Second- and Third-Order Effects:**

- **Increased Incarceration and System Strain:** A near-900 annual increase in fentanyl-related felony incarcerations could strain prison capacity, impacting budgets and potentially crowding out inmates needing psychiatric or substance-use disorder support.

- **Displacement of Courts and Law Enforcement Priorities:** Courts may face backlog challenges, and law enforcement could shift attention disproportionately toward low-level possessions to meet felony prosecution targets, affecting their ability to tackle high-level trafficking.

- **Potential Exacerbation of Health and Social Inequities:** Criminalizing possession even at low levels may disproportionately affect marginalized communities with higher addiction prevalence or limited access to treatment, entrenching cycles of incarceration and health disparities.

- **Reduced Trust and Engagement with Public Health Services:** Harsh penalties may discourage users or witnesses from seeking emergency help during overdoses due to fear of felony charges, complicating overdose reversal efforts.

- **Political Polarization on Drug Policy:** Passage could deepend factions in Colorado politics between punitive and health-oriented approaches, complicating future bipartisan initiatives focused on comprehensive drug policy reform.

**4. Unintended Consequences and Implementation Risks:**

- **Treatment Access Bottleneck:** Without funding to scale treatment programs, courts’ increased authority to order treatment may lead to long waitlists and default incarceration, undermining rehabilitative aims.

- **Potential Judicial and Prosecutorial Discretion Overload:** Judges and prosecutors will face complex decisions that could vary widely, resulting in inconsistent application of the treatment provision and potential legal challenges.

- **Budget Overruns and Opportunity Costs:** With limited new funds, the measure’s incarceration costs could force cuts in other critical areas—public health, education, community safety—hampering holistic crisis responses.

- **Risk of Net-Widening:** Expanding felony classifications to any fentanyl possession may sweep in low-level users or incidental possessors who traditionally would receive diversion or lower-level penalties, increasing criminal justice involvement unnecessarily.

- **Data and Evaluation Gaps:** The absence of explicit monitoring, evaluation, or sunset provisions risks difficulties in assessing whether the measure achieves desired outcomes, impeding data-informed adjustments.

**5. Non-Obvious Implications for Decision-Makers:**

- **Balancing Enforcement and Public Health Requires Integrated Funding:** Mandating treatment eligibility without allocating fiscal resources could create a mismatched policy—courts ordered to divert users to treatment that is not sufficiently available, deepening justice system burdens instead of alleviating drug harms.

- **Penalties vs. Prevention Tradeoff:** Decision-makers should weigh whether emphasizing supply-side criminalization detracts from upstream prevention and education efforts that might produce longer-term harm reduction.

- **Judicial System as Policy Lever but Also Bottleneck:** Enhancing courts’ role in ordering treatment could be positive if program capacity exists, but without systematic guidelines and resources, this shift may inadvertently intensify judicial backlogs and inequalities in access to alternatives to incarceration.

- **Potential for Policy Feedback Against Overdose Mitigation Efforts:** Harsh penalties might inadvertently suppress behaviors crucial to overdose survival (calling emergency services), suggesting a need for complementary legal protections (e.g., Good Samaritan laws) or clearer messaging to avoid undermining public health goals.

- **Future Legislative and Budgetary Flexibility May Be Limited:** Once penal codes are hardened via ballot measures, it becomes harder for legislatures to adapt policies responsively without complex repeal or amendment processes, underscoring the importance of built-in review mechanisms or sunset clauses.

---

**Summary:**  
The Colorado fentanyl penalties ballot measure represents a significant escalation in drug law enforcement aiming to address a severe overdose crisis reflected in high fentanyl death counts. Evidence supports the measure’s projected increases in felony convictions and incarceration costs but also highlights critical implementation risks—primarily inadequate treatment capacity and budget constraints. Experience and research suggest punitive approaches alone may not curb overdose deaths and could have counterproductive effects on public health and equity. Decision-makers should consider this measure in the context of systemic resource tradeoffs, judicial capacity, and the complex behavioral dynamics of addiction to ensure complementary investments in treatment and harm reduction are prioritized to achieve lasting impact.
